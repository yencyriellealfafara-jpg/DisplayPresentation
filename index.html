PART I

Elements of Design

The Fundamental Components

Line

Line: A mark made by a moving point across a surface. It has length and width, and it can be straight, curved, jagged, thick, or thin.

Shape & Form

Shape & Form: A shape is a two-dimensional area defined by lines. A form is the three-dimensional version, possessing depth, volume, and mass.

Color

Color: The visual sensation produced by the way an object reflects or emits light, categorized by its hue, value, and saturation.

Texture

Texture: The perceived surface quality of an artwork or design. It can be physical or tactile, or visual and implied.

Space

Space: The area around, within, and between elements in a design. It is divided into positive space and negative space.

Value

Value: The relative lightness or darkness of a color or tone, independent of its hue. It creates contrast and defines shapes.

Elements of Design in Practice: Visual Examples

Tactile texture refers to the actual physical surface quality of an object that can be felt by touch. Examples include the rough grain of natural wood, the soft pile of velvet fabric, or the cold, smooth surface of polished marble.

Visual texture, or implied texture, is the illusion of physical texture created through artistic techniques. In design, this is achieved by using patterns, varying line weights, or shading to simulate the appearance of surfaces like stone, metal, or fur on a two-dimensional plane. These visual cues allow the viewer to perceive depth and material quality without physical contact.

Principles of Design

Governing Rules & Guidelines

Balance

Balance: The distribution of visual weight within a design. It creates a sense of equilibrium, stability, and comfort for the viewer.

Contrast

Contrast: The arrangement of opposite or strongly dissimilar elements. It breaks monotony, draws attention to focal points, and makes content readable.

Emphasis

Emphasis: The practice of making a specific element stand out as the most important part of the design, capturing the viewer's attention first.

Movement

Movement: The path the viewer's eye follows through a design. It creates a sense of flow and narrative, leading the audience smoothly.

Unity & Harmony

Unity and Harmony: The feeling of completeness and cohesion, where all individual elements look like they belong together and serve a single purpose.

Rhythm & Pattern

Rhythm and Pattern: Pattern is the consistent repetition of a specific element. Rhythm is the feeling of organized movement created by the repetition.

FOCAL POINT

The best example of design elements and principles in practice is a balanced composition where symmetry creates stability and a clear focal point captures attention.

Activity: “Design the House”

The students will be divided into four groups. Each group will be given a picture of an empty house. Each group will have 1 minute and 30 seconds to add or draw details to the house using their own creative ideas and applying the elements of good design.

After the time is up, each group will choose one representative to present and explain their group’s design to the class.

Questions

01.

Visual Attention

Look around the room or at a common poster or advertisement. Which single element, such as color, line, or space, grabs your attention first, and why do you think the creator designed it that way?

02.

Lack of Contrast

Imagine a website or a poster where all the text is the exact same size, color, and font, with zero spacing or contrast. How would that affect your ability to understand its message?

03.

Stability vs Chaos

If you were asked to design a poster that feels stable and professional versus one that feels chaotic and energetic, which principles of design would you manipulate, and how would you use them?

VISUAL HIERARCHY

Synthesis: Mastering Visual Order

The elements of design serve as the foundational building blocks of any visual work, providing the raw materials like lines, shapes, colors, textures, spaces, and values.

However, raw materials alone do not make a successful composition. The principles of design such as balance, contrast, emphasis, movement, unity, and rhythm act as the governing rules that guide how those elements are organized.

Ultimately, mastering both elements and principles allows creators to move beyond random arrangement, enabling them to intentionally guide the viewer's eye, evoke specific emotions, communicate clear messages, and transform visually chaotic concepts into polished, professional, and harmonious designs.
